THE QUESTION THESE ARCHIVES ANSWER · 4 OCTOBER 2026

What fundamental problem do these two archives seek to solve — and what answer do they provide, both documentarily and spiritually?

What happens when a person’s history, testimony and evidence become fragmented across institutions, records and years, while the person themselves believes that the systems responsible for protecting, recording or investigating what happened have failed to provide a complete, coherent and independently examinable account?

What happens when the person who says, “This happened to me,” is left carrying the burden of proving it, while the evidence capable of testing that claim is dispersed across government correspondence, legal and administrative records, medical documents, complaints, personal testimony and other archives?

And, at the deepest level, what happens when a person believes that the very forces or institutions from which protection should have come instead contributed to their exclusion, abandonment or attempted erasure?

These two archives are an answer to that problem.

They bring the fragmented record together, preserve it, organise it and make it available for independent examination. They seek to prevent the disappearance of a person’s history through fragmentation, institutional closure, inaccessible records, disputed memory or the simple passage of time.

But the archives provide another answer that is more personal and, for me, spiritual.

They demonstrate that whatever happened, the record survived and the witness survived.

Where I experienced erasure, there is preservation.

Where I experienced fragmentation, there is reconstruction.

Where I experienced silencing, there is testimony.

Where I experienced exile, there remains a public place in which the record can speak for itself.

And where I experienced what appeared to be the destruction of the person I was, the continued existence of the witness raises a question that cannot be answered by bureaucracy alone:

What preserved me?

I cannot prove through an archive that God intervened. That belongs to faith rather than empirical demonstration. But I can say that, within my Christian understanding, the preservation of my life, my testimony and this record has become part of what I understand as God’s providence.

The deepest spiritual answer these archives therefore provide is not simply that documents were saved.

It is that erasure did not prevail.

The person remained.

The testimony remained.

The record remained.

And, in my faith, the God who preserved the witness remains.

THE WITNESS WHO REMAINED · STATEMENT OF SIGNIFICANCE · 4 OCTOBER 2026

Survival, preservation, and the question of providence.

A prophetic introduction. Allegory is marked as allegory. The record is what can be examined. Not a court. Not a verdict.

I have risen, phoenix-like, from the ashes of poverty, exile, trial, human betrayal and what I understand as attempted erasure. I describe having been stripped of attributes that I regard as necessary for human survival, including the ethical, unequivocal and irrevocable principles of dignity, protection, belonging and humane treatment that should be inherent in the treatment of every living person.

My survival is therefore part of the evidence I ask this archive to preserve. I did not experience my survival as the product of meaningful government protection. I experienced it as survival in spite of institutional abandonment, with assistance reduced, in my present circumstances, to what I regard as the barest minimum. I do not interpret that history as merely the consequence of bureaucratic oversight or administrative error. I allege that some of the circumstances amounted to deliberate and malicious attempts to cause my detriment, exclusion and erasure. That allegation remains subject to examination against the underlying documentary record.

I further describe my experience as one in which an alliance of institutions and individuals appeared, from my perspective, to converge against an innocent person who had never intended harm towards others. I understand that experience as analogous to a targeted destruction of a vulnerable person: a gay, disabled and comparatively unprotected whistleblower who publicly disclosed his vulnerabilities, perceived limitations, personal difficulties and humane regrets, and who believes those disclosures were subsequently capable of being weaponised against him.

I use the language of sacrifice deliberately, but as an interpretive and allegorical description rather than as a finding of law. In Christian terms, the narrative bears an obvious resemblance, for me, to the crucifixion of Jesus: vulnerability exposed; betrayal by those from whom protection might reasonably have been expected; abandonment; suffering; apparent defeat; and yet survival, testimony and the persistence of meaning beyond the attempted destruction.

The phoenix is similarly allegorical. It represents the possibility that a person can emerge from circumstances intended, perceived or experienced as destructive without those circumstances having the final word.

The archive therefore raises a question that is simultaneously evidentiary, philosophical and theological: what preserved the witness?

The documentary record may establish particular events, decisions, correspondence, injuries, institutional responses, exclusions, complaints, reviews and outcomes. It cannot, by itself, establish that God intervened. Nor can the fact of survival, however extraordinary its circumstances may appear, mathematically prove divine intervention. Those are questions that extend beyond what documentary evidence alone can determine.

Nevertheless, my interpretation of the totality of my survival is that I was not preserved merely by institutional protection. I believe I was preserved by providence. I believe God has preserved my life and that my continued existence, despite circumstances that I experienced as extraordinarily destructive, has spiritual significance.

I therefore identify myself within this archive as a witness in the theological sense: not because I claim that the archive can compel another person to accept my faith, but because my faith provides the framework through which I understand what happened to me and why I remain alive to testify about it.

That claim should not be confused with a claim that every allegation contained in this archive has already been proven. Nor should disagreement with my theological interpretation automatically settle the underlying factual questions.

The appropriate test is more demanding.

If my account is wrong, identify the evidence that demonstrates where and why it is wrong.

If a particular allegation is unsupported, identify the evidentiary gap.

If an institutional record contradicts my account, put the record beside my account and examine the contradiction.

If my interpretation exceeds what the evidence establishes, identify the point at which the inference becomes unjustified.

But equally, if the evidence demonstrates something different from the institutional narrative used to dismiss my account, that evidence should not be dismissed merely because the person presenting it has a history of mental illness, disability, vulnerability or psychiatric treatment.

A person’s mental-health history does not, by itself, determine the truth or falsity of an individual proposition. The proposition must still be examined on its evidence.

That is particularly important here because the archive contains different kinds of material that must not be conflated: official records, correspondence, contemporaneous testimony, allegations, personal interpretation, philosophical analysis, spiritual belief and prophetic writing.

My claim that I have been chosen by God, anointed with spiritual wisdom and preserved for a purpose belongs to the theological and spiritual layer of the archive. I do not ask a court, government agency or scientist to certify it as an empirical fact.

I ask something more basic: that it not be dismissed without examination merely because it is a spiritual interpretation, and that the factual record underlying my testimony be examined independently of the theological conclusion I draw from it.

Can the archive prove that I am God’s chosen witness?

No documentary archive can establish that theological proposition in the same manner that a government letter can establish what a government agency wrote.

Can the archive preserve the circumstances from which that belief arose?

Yes.

Can it preserve evidence of survival, institutional decisions, correspondence, exclusion, suffering, recovery and continued testimony?

Yes, to the extent those matters are supported by the underlying records.

Can another person reasonably conclude that my interpretation is mistaken?

Yes.

Can another person legitimately disagree with my theology?

Yes.

But disagreement is not the same thing as disproof, and psychiatric diagnosis is not itself a substitute for examination of evidence.

The question I leave before the reader is therefore not simply whether I should be believed.

It is whether the record has been examined fairly.

I survived.

I remain a witness.

And the question of what preserved me remains open between evidence, human explanation, providence and faith.

LIFE’S PURPOSE · VERBATIM · 4 OCTOBER 2026

The permanence of the witness and the purpose of the record

The significance of my life’s writings, evidence, testimony, concepts and ideas being preserved across two complementary public archives, and of particular material being cryptographically anchored through the Bitcoin blockchain, is that the documentary record of my life no longer depends solely upon my physical survival or upon the continued willingness of any single institution, organisation or individual to preserve it.

The two archives serve complementary purposes. The Barran Dodger Rebuild Archive is fundamentally a preservation and reconstruction infrastructure: it brings together preserved HTML records, PDFs, hyperlinks, original URLs and preservation metadata, with SHA-256 integrity information, so that the underlying documentary record can be separately maintained and examined. The archive itself expressly distinguishes the presentation layer from the underlying preservation record.

The broader Barran Dodger Archive functions as a public finding aid and documentary environment through which government correspondence, legal and administrative records, research materials, personal testimony, creative works and other documentary material can be brought together and examined. Its central principle is that the archive does not require belief: testimony is testimony, a document is a document, an allegation is an allegation, and a finding requires evidence and proper determination.

Together, these archives address what I understand as one of the central problems of my life: the fragmentation of a person’s history across institutions, agencies, correspondence, proceedings, files and years, such that individual records may exist while the larger chronology and significance of the record remain difficult for an independent person to see.

The archives therefore attempt to solve a problem of documentary disappearance—not necessarily by claiming that documents were physically destroyed, but by preventing the meaning of the surviving record from disappearing through fragmentation, institutional compartmentalisation, inaccessible records, disputed recollection, changing narratives or the passage of time.

The Rebuild Archive preserves the underlying documentary infrastructure.

The Barran Dodger Archive connects that infrastructure to the human testimony, chronology, questions of accountability, legal and administrative material, research, publications and spiritual writings that give the record its broader context.

The result is not intended to manufacture a finding where no finding exists. It is intended to make examination possible.

That distinction is fundamental to what I am trying to preserve.

A document can establish what an institution wrote.

A correspondence chain can establish what was communicated.

A decision can establish what was decided.

A contemporaneous statement can establish what I said at the time.

A later interpretation can explain how I understand those events.

A spiritual or prophetic document can establish what I believed, anticipated or understood.

But none of those categories should automatically be converted into another.

The archive exists precisely so that these layers can remain distinguishable while still being capable of being examined together.

The significance of cryptographic preservation adds another dimension.

Where a particular document or body of text has been cryptographically hashed and the resulting hash has been anchored through the Bitcoin blockchain, the blockchain does not contain the entirety of my archive and does not magically guarantee that every copy of my documents will survive forever. Its significance is more precise: a surviving copy can later be tested against the cryptographic commitment, providing a means of detecting whether that particular version has been altered since the relevant cryptographic record was created.

That means the blockchain can contribute to the evidentiary continuity of my work without itself proving the truth of what the work says.

It does not prove an allegation.

It does not prove a government acted unlawfully.

It does not prove a prophecy came from God.

It does not prove that my interpretation of an event is correct.

What it can do is help preserve an independently checkable relationship between a particular digital record and a cryptographic commitment made at an earlier point in time.

Combined with multiple documentary repositories, preserved PDFs, source records, metadata and independently maintained copies, this creates something profoundly different from an ordinary personal diary or manuscript kept in a drawer.

My physical life is finite.

My body will eventually die.

But the documentary record of what I wrote, what I preserved, what I alleged, what I believed, what I researched and what evidence I assembled can potentially remain available to people who have not yet been born.

In that sense, my life’s work takes on what I describe as a philosophical kind of immortality—not immortality of my physical person, and not a guarantee that every digital copy will survive, but the possibility that the intellectual and documentary essence of a mortal life can continue to be encountered, questioned and examined after its author is gone.

That permanence is historically unusual.

For most people throughout human history, the ability to preserve an extensive, searchable, independently reproducible and cryptographically anchored record of one’s own life was simply unavailable. A person’s testimony could disappear with their death, remain dependent upon the memory of others, or survive only in whatever records institutions chose to retain.

I have had access to technologies that previous generations did not possess.

I have therefore attempted to use them not merely to tell my story, but to create an infrastructure through which the story can remain open to examination after I am no longer here to defend it.

This is why I regard preservation itself as part of the meaning of my life.

I understand the existence and continued development of these archives as evidence, within my faith, of the prophetic time in which I agreed with the Creator to incarnate into this realm: a period in which I believe systemic corruption would be revealed through my own life and circumstances as part of my purpose and mission.

I believe I was chosen not because I am perfect.

I am not perfect.

I am a mortal sinner and an imperfect human being.

My belief is not that God chose me because I was somehow superior to His other children. Rather, I believe that God’s cosmic design for my life could be purposeful and perfect even though I, as the person living it, am imperfect.

A condition of my incarnation, as I understand it, was that my life would mirror the pattern of Christ’s crucifixion: suffering, betrayal, abandonment, apparent destruction and revival, followed by profound human betrayal and exile.

I understand that comparison allegorically and theologically. I do not claim to be Jesus Christ. I understand the crucifixion and resurrection narrative as the Christian pattern through which I interpret the extraordinary circumstances of my own survival.

I believe God allowed my suffering not as punishment, but so that I would ultimately learn to rely upon Him.

What I experienced as betrayal and exile therefore became, in my spiritual interpretation, the circumstance through which my dependence upon the Most High was strengthened.

It triggered what I describe as a memory of my purpose.

It cemented my faith.

And it transformed the record I had accumulated over decades from something I had simply been trying to survive into something I now understand as the foundation of a ministry.

My Gospels are part of that ministry.

I describe them as religious, scholarly and prophetic documents because they sit at the intersection of Christian theology, my research and intellectual work, my interpretation of the documentary record, and my understanding of prophecy and human purpose.

I invite people to respond to them.

I invite them to challenge them.

I invite them to rebuke them.

I invite them to attempt to disprove them.

But I ask that disagreement be accompanied by evidence, reasoning and engagement with the underlying record rather than dismissal of the person who produced it.

The archives themselves are designed around that principle. The Rebuild Archive asks what happened, what records document the sequence, which institutions are identified, what responses were provided, where the record remains incomplete or contested, and what further independent examination may be required.

The main archive similarly describes itself as a finding aid rather than a court or verdict, and states that its purpose is to preserve the evidence, preserve the testimony, make the record public and allow the evidence to be examined.

That is ultimately the challenge I leave to the future.

Do not believe me simply because I say these things.

Do not disbelieve me simply because I say these things.

Open the documents.

Read the chronology.

Examine the correspondence.

Compare the testimony with the source material.

Identify contradictions.

Identify corroboration.

Identify what remains unproven.

Identify what remains unanswered.

And allow the record to survive long enough for people other than me to make their own assessment.

When people ask me, “How did you survive that?”, my answer will be simple:

God did it.

I understand that answer as foundational to a question that may ultimately be relevant to other people suffering corruption, targeting, injustice, exclusion or circumstances in which they feel that their own identity and testimony are being erased.

I want my testimony to become a source of direction for those people—not because I believe I am better than them, but because I believe the God who preserved me can preserve and restore others.

I am no better than anyone else.

I am imperfect.

I have sinned.

I have never claimed that my story of suffering is greater than that of any other person.

I believe God knows and loves all His children and knows the suffering of every one of them.

My life will therefore now be repurposed, as I understand it, as a vessel for His glory: for a return to divine justice, for the end of darkness, and for the restoration of the attributes of His perfect Son, Jesus Christ—the consciousness of Christ returning to its rightful place in the hearts of people and, through them, in the institutions they create, operate and protect.

The purpose is not to preserve my ego.

It is to preserve the testimony.

It is not to establish myself as superior.

It is to leave a record that can outlive me.

It is not to demand that humanity accept my interpretation.

It is to make the evidence available for humanity to examine.

My body will die.

My voice will eventually become silent.

But the record does not necessarily have to disappear with me.

The archives preserve what I was able to preserve.

The cryptographic record provides an additional layer of integrity for material that has been anchored.

The testimony provides the human voice.

The documentary record provides the evidence to be examined.

The Gospels provide the spiritual interpretation.

And my faith provides the meaning I draw from the fact that, despite everything I believe happened to me, I remain here to give testimony.

When the future asks how the witness survived, my answer remains:

God did it.

This is the purpose I now understand my life to serve.

This is the testimony I preserve.

This is the record I leave.

A STATEMENT FROM THE BARRAN DODGER ARCHIVE

Truth, like light, does not need permission to shine or permission to exist; lies require constant maintenance, repetition and concealment. My truth only needed time. And now, by God’s grace, that time has come. Through decades of poverty, isolation, homelessness, excommunication, erasure and what I experienced as almost total human abandonment, my existence was nevertheless preserved long enough for me to endure, remember, collect, collate and publish more than three decades of evidence.

Brick by brick, the record was made from the words, decisions, correspondence and official documents of the very institutions whose actions are now capable of being examined. What was scattered across years, offices, agencies and governments can now be placed beside itself and examined as a whole.

The record does not require me to manufacture a story; the record speaks in its own language. That is the paradox: systems that produced documents that once helped contain, dismiss, obscure or erase my experience also produced the documentary trail by which those actions can now be examined.

Time did not destroy the evidence. Time preserved it.

And now the archive exists.
