The Enemy Within: A Lifetime of Family Betrayal, Exile, Survival and Divine Purpose

The Child Who Had to Survive Alone: Unequal Treatment, Imagination, Art and the Contradictions of Family Belonging

To understand the significance of my later exile from The Family, it is necessary to look back at the child I was and the circumstances in which I grew up. My experience was not simply one of feeling different. I experienced a sustained pattern of being treated differently, exposed to narcissism, abuse and neglect, and left to find ways of surviving emotionally when the family environment did not provide the security, protection and recognition I needed. Looking back, I believe that I was required to develop an inner world because the outer world of my childhood did not consistently offer me a place where I felt safe, valued or understood.

I had to survive, in many respects, on my own imagination. That imagination became more than an escape. It became a place where I could create meaning, develop an identity and preserve parts of myself that I did not feel were recognised by the people around me. In circumstances where I experienced rejection or neglect, art offered another way of existing. It allowed me to transform experience into expression, to imagine possibilities beyond the limitations of my immediate environment, and to retain a sense of individuality when I felt that my place in the family was conditional.

I now see a connection between that childhood experience and the life I subsequently pursued through art, writing, intellectual work and the preservation of my own testimony. The imagination that helped me survive as a child became part of the means through which I later examined my life, documented my experiences and attempted to make sense of events that had previously seemed disconnected. What may have appeared to others as difference was also, for me, a way of maintaining an inner life when I did not feel that I could rely on the family around me to provide the recognition and protection I needed.

At eighteen, I left home and lived on the dole in a squat. That is a significant fact in my personal history because it demonstrates the degree of independence I was required to develop at an early age. I was not entering adulthood from a position of equal security or support. I was trying to survive outside the family home, relying on limited resources and my own ability to adapt. I had to work out how to live without the ordinary assurances that a young person might expect from their family.

The contrast with my sister, whom I experienced as the golden child, is central to my understanding of the unequal treatment within the family. I cannot imagine that she would ever have been expected to survive or live under the same conditions. In my experience, the expectations, protections and opportunities afforded to us were profoundly different. I was the child who had to find a way through hardship, while I understood my sister to occupy a position within the family that carried a different level of acceptance and security.

The significance is not merely that one child had a more comfortable experience than another. It is that unequal treatment can shape a person’s understanding of their own worth, their place in the family and what they can expect from other people. When one child experiences neglect, rejection and early independence while another appears protected, the difference can become a defining feature of the family relationship. For me, it contributed to the feeling that I had never been granted the same unquestioned right to belong.

I carried that history into adulthood. I continued to seek connection, to offer kindness and to maintain the hope that family belonging might eventually be restored. Yet I also carried the knowledge that I had already survived without the family support that I believed should have been available to me. That contradiction became increasingly important as I tried to understand the later estrangement: I had been required to live independently from a young age, but the loss of family belonging continued to have consequences well into my adult life.

Phillip Island: The Day the Extended Family Unexpectedly Appeared

One event that has acquired particular significance for me in retrospect occurred when I became engaged to Shane, my much younger fiancé, at Phillip Island. I casually mentioned to Mum that she should come down. What happened next was unusual enough that I have continued to think about it: the entire extended family ended up turning up on that one day, in circumstances that I remember as absurd, unexpected and entirely out of character.

There had been no invitation, as I understood it, that would explain why everyone would arrive together in this way. I had casually mentioned to my mother that she should come down, yet the result was that the extended family appeared to gather around an event in my life. The scale and timing of their attendance seemed disproportionate to the casual nature of the invitation. Looking back, I find it difficult to reconcile that gathering with the wider history of exclusion and unequal treatment I experienced.

At the time, it might have been interpreted as an expression of family support. The family had appeared, after all, at a significant moment in my life. Yet the event has become more complicated in my memory because it sits against the history of my childhood, the differences in treatment between my sister and me, and the later rupture that culminated in my exclusion from The Family.

I have wondered whether their unexpected appearance reflected guilt about the way I had been treated, discomfort about the possibility of losing me, or a desire to demonstrate support in a way that did not require anyone to acknowledge the underlying problems. I have even wondered, looking back, whether the gathering represented an unusual attempt to compensate for the treatment I had experienced or whether it had some connection to decisions about my eventual exclusion.

I cannot establish from the gathering alone what motivated each person to attend, and I do not want to present speculation about their private intentions as a proven fact. What I can say is that the event remains striking because of the contrast it presents: a family that could appear together at a significant moment in my life, yet whose relationships with me later became defined by estrangement, silence and exclusion.

That contradiction is what gives Phillip Island its significance in my personal narrative. The gathering appeared, on the surface, to show family unity and support. In retrospect, it raises questions for me about what that unity meant, what was understood but never said, and how a family could collectively appear to acknowledge my life at one moment while I later experienced such a profound loss of belonging.

Nana, Family Separation and the Inheritance Questions

My memories of Nana have become another important part of my attempt to understand the family history. I have come to question whether the treatment I experienced reflected a broader pattern of family relationships in which people who were strong, independent or difficult to control became isolated from the support and companionship they needed.

In retrospect, I have wondered whether Nana experienced a similar kind of exclusion. I have questioned whether she was separated from people and relationships that mattered to her, whether her circumstances were managed in ways that served other people’s interests, and whether decisions concerning her estate and inheritance were handled in accordance with her wishes.

I have raised concerns about my father’s sister, Aunty Bev, and Ian, whom I understood to be a lawyer, including questions about their roles, their access to Nana and the possible financial consequences of decisions made around her life and death. I have also questioned whether an inheritance that I believed should have been available was withheld or otherwise dealt with in a manner inconsistent with Nana’s wishes.

These are matters that require evidence of the relevant relationships, financial arrangements, estate documents and decisions. I cannot establish financial abuse, improper influence or entitlement merely from the fact that people were involved in Nana’s affairs or that I experienced the outcome as unjust. But I can record the questions I have carried and identify the material needed to assess them.

What makes these questions so painful is my recollection of the difference between the way Nana was treated in life and the praise that could be offered after her death. I remember being asked to deliver her eulogy, and I remember the praise associated with my contribution to her life. That recognition meant something to me because my relationship with Nana mattered.

Yet the praise also sharpened a contradiction I have struggled to understand. If my contribution to Nana’s life was valued, why did I so often feel excluded or unsupported within the wider family? If the family could recognise my love for her at the time of her death, why was it so difficult for me to feel that my own life and suffering received comparable consideration?

I have wondered whether Nana and I shared experiences of being misunderstood, marginalised or excluded. I do not claim that our circumstances were identical. I recognise that her life and my life must be understood on their own evidence. But I cannot ignore the possibility that examining her history might illuminate questions about family relationships, money, control and belonging that have also shaped my own.

I also remember the circumstances in which other family members travelled to Europe for an extended holiday while I remained at home with my fiancé, whom I experienced as abusive and exploitative. The contrast between their travel and my circumstances remains painful to me. I felt isolated and exposed while others appeared able to enjoy comfort and opportunity.

I cannot infer from the holiday alone that anyone intended to abandon or endanger me. But the event forms part of the context in which I experienced a profound imbalance between the freedom and security available to other people and the difficulties I was trying to survive.

I want the questions surrounding Nana’s care, relationships and estate to be examined fairly. I also want her life to be remembered as more than an inheritance dispute or a source of conflict. She was a person whose relationships mattered, whose wishes deserved respect and whose history should not be reduced to the interests of those who survived her.

The Shame of a Perfect Public Facade

My family may disagree with my interpretation of our history. They may believe that their conduct was justified, that they did what they could, or that my recollections fail to capture their perspective. They are entitled to respond to the specific matters I have raised.

But they cannot resolve the moral questions simply by maintaining the appearance of a successful family.

The public image of a family is not the same thing as the private truth of its relationships. Travel, comfortable homes, professional achievements, social standing and outward respectability cannot establish that every family member has been treated fairly. Nor can they erase the consequences experienced by someone who believes that they were excluded, neglected or left to survive without adequate support.

If my family believes its public reputation accurately represents the way it has treated me, then it should be able to account for the history I have described. It should be able to explain the difference in the circumstances in which my sister and I entered adulthood. It should be able to address the periods in which I was homeless or living in poverty, the repeated crises I experienced, the relationships I have described as harmful, and the questions I have raised about Nana’s circumstances.

It should be able to distinguish what it knew from what it did not know, what it could reasonably have done from what was beyond its control, and what it regrets from what it continues to defend.

I am not demanding that anyone perform guilt for the benefit of my audience. I am demanding that the appearance of virtue not be treated as a substitute for accountability.

If my family has acted honourably, a careful examination of the facts should help demonstrate that. If it has made mistakes, an honest response would acknowledge them. If its members believe my account is mistaken, they can identify the particular claims they dispute and explain why. None of those responses requires humiliation, retaliation or public abuse.

What I cannot accept is the idea that the person who has experienced the consequences should remain silent merely to protect the comfort, status or reputation of those who do not share them.

The shame I describe is not a verdict I can impose on another person’s conscience. It is the moral discomfort that may arise when people are asked to compare their public values with their private conduct. If there is a discrepancy, it is for those involved to confront it honestly.

I have had to confront the discrepancy between the family I hoped I had and the relationships I experienced. My family must decide whether it is willing to confront the questions my testimony raises.

The Price of Integrity

There is a price to refusing to remain silent when silence would be more convenient. I have experienced that price in relationships, financial circumstances, social belonging and the struggle to establish a stable life. I have also experienced the cost of continuing to hope that people might change, even when my history gave me reason to question whether they would.

I once believed that love, patience and forgiveness might eventually lead to understanding. I believed that if I continued to explain myself, my family might recognise the person behind the conflict and the circumstances behind the distress. I believed that a future might exist in which we could acknowledge what had happened without requiring me to deny my own experience.

I have had to reconsider that hope. Forgiveness, in my faith, does not require me to pretend that harm did not occur. Love does not require me to remain in relationships that I experience as damaging. And the desire for reconciliation does not require me to surrender my right to describe the past or preserve the evidence relevant to it.

I can leave ultimate judgment to God while continuing to ask human beings to account for their decisions. I can refuse revenge while refusing silence. I can recognise that I cannot know every person’s private motive while still insisting that actions, omissions and documented decisions be examined for their consequences.

I do not need to destroy another person’s life to tell the truth about my own. I do not need to invent certainty where the evidence leaves questions unresolved. I do not need to imitate the conduct I believe harmed me in order to insist that my experience matters.

My integrity depends on maintaining those distinctions. I want the record to be strong enough to withstand scrutiny, including scrutiny of my own conclusions. I want the documents to be available, the chronology to be coherent and the difference between evidence and interpretation to remain clear.

That is the opposite of erasure. It is the decision to leave a record that can be read, questioned and assessed independently.

They Expected My Story to End

For a long time, I felt as though other people had come to regard my circumstances as permanent: as though poverty, illness, isolation and crisis would define the boundaries of my life indefinitely. I felt that my possibilities were being measured by the most difficult periods I had endured, rather than by my capacity to recover, create, learn and continue.

I cannot know what every person expected of my future. But I know how it felt to be living through repeated hardship without reliable support, and I know how easily a person’s suffering can become the only story others are willing to hear about them.

I am still here.

I continued to write. I continued to create. I continued to preserve documents, reconstruct timelines and attempt to understand the history of my life. I continued to seek a way to make my experiences intelligible beyond the immediate relationships and institutions through which they had unfolded.

My archive is part of that effort. It is not a substitute for independent verification, and it does not make every interpretation automatically correct. Its purpose is to preserve material that can be examined, compared and assessed. It gives readers the opportunity to look beyond my conclusions and consider the underlying records for themselves.

My survival does not prove every allegation I have made. But neither does the existence of disputed interpretations erase the fact that I have a history, that I have documented experiences and that I am entitled to ask serious questions about the treatment I received.

The record should be judged by what it contains. The claims should be assessed against the evidence. The unanswered questions should remain visible until they can be answered. And the person whose life is represented in those documents should not disappear behind the language used to describe him.

The Final Word

To those whom I believe contributed to my suffering, my message is simple: you do not have to agree with my interpretation of every event, but you should not assume that your silence will determine how my life is remembered.

To the institutions whose records I have preserved, I ask that your decisions be examined against the responsibilities you were required to uphold. To the professionals who dealt with me, I ask that your actions and reasoning be considered in their proper context. To my former fiancé and the people involved in the relationships I have described, I ask that the evidence be allowed to speak without intimidation, distortion or convenient omissions.

And to my blood family, I ask something more personal.

Look beyond the public image. Look beyond the family story that has allowed everyone to continue with their lives. Look at the child who left home at eighteen and had to survive on his own. Look at the adult who continued to seek acceptance, even after repeated disappointment. Look at the person who wanted to be loved by his family and who kept returning because he believed that, one day, they might understand him.

Consider what it means when a person spends years trying to earn a place that they believed should have been theirs without conditions. Consider what it means when someone continues to hope for reconciliation while feeling that their experiences are being dismissed. Consider what it means when a person has to build an archive of his own life because he fears that his account will otherwise be ignored, misrepresented or lost.

You may remember the past differently. You may reject my interpretation. But if you believe that I am wrong, answer the specific questions. Address the evidence. Explain the decisions. Do not confuse the preservation of a respectable appearance with the resolution of the underlying history.

I cannot compel you to love me. I cannot compel you to understand me. I cannot make you acknowledge what I believe you have failed to acknowledge. And I cannot recover the years I spent hoping that our relationships would become what I needed them to be.

What I can do is stop making my existence dependent on your approval.

I can stop waiting for permission to tell my story. I can stop confusing the possibility of reconciliation with an obligation to remain silent. I can stop treating the absence of an explanation as proof that I should abandon the questions.

If there is shame to confront, each person must confront it through their own conscience and their own conduct. If there are mistakes to acknowledge, those involved must decide whether they are prepared to acknowledge them. If there is evidence of wrongdoing, it should be examined through appropriate and independent processes. If my conclusions are mistaken in particular respects, the record should permit that to be demonstrated as well.

I leave ultimate judgment to God. I do not seek revenge. I seek truth, accountability, dignity and the freedom to live without having to prove my humanity to the people who were supposed to recognise it.

My family may continue with its life. Its members may travel, maintain their homes, enjoy their relationships and preserve whatever public image they consider important. None of those things is, by itself, proof of wrongdoing. But neither is any of it an answer to the questions I have raised about my own life.

I am no longer willing to measure my worth by whether I am included in their world.

I was a child who wanted to be loved. I became an adult who learned to survive without the support I believed I should have received. I became an artist who used imagination to give expression to an inner life that had too often gone unrecognised. I became a writer who tried to make sense of experience. And I became the keeper of a documentary record because I wanted my history to remain available for examination.

That is my response to exclusion: not to disappear, but to preserve my voice.

That is my response to betrayal as I experienced it: not to seek revenge, but to insist that the record remain.

That is my response to the loss of belonging: to build a life whose meaning does not depend on whether my family welcomes me back.

I gave my family years of patience, love and opportunities for reconciliation because I believed those relationships could change. I cannot know what the future holds, but I no longer need to sacrifice my dignity in the hope that someone else will finally recognise it.

The child who had to survive alone has become the adult who can tell his own story. The person who once searched for acceptance now understands that belonging cannot be secured by repeatedly abandoning himself. The man who once hoped that silence would eventually be replaced by understanding has chosen to preserve a record that can be examined beyond the boundaries of the family.

My testimony is not a demand that the world accept every conclusion without question. It is a demand that my life, my records and my experiences be considered seriously, honestly and in context.

I am still here. I am still creating. I am still documenting. I am still bearing witness.

My faith remains. My conscience matters. My life has value beyond the judgments of my family, the decisions of institutions or the public image of people who may never agree with my account.

I will not claim to know what every person intended. I will not pretend that every unresolved question has already been answered. But I will not allow uncertainty about another person’s motives to erase the experiences I remember or the records I have preserved.

The truth deserves examination. The vulnerable deserve dignity. Family relationships deserve honesty. Public institutions deserve scrutiny. And no person’s humanity should depend on whether those with greater power find it convenient to recognise them.

I wanted to be accepted and loved. I wanted my family to understand me. I wanted to believe that compassion would eventually overcome the differences that had separated us.

I cannot make those hopes come true by myself.

What I can do is tell the truth as I understand it, preserve the evidence, acknowledge the limits of what I can prove, and continue to live according to the faith and integrity that have sustained me.

They may have their own account of who I am. I have mine, supported by the records I have preserved and open to independent examination.

I am not asking for permission to exist.

I am not asking for permission to remember.

I am not asking for permission to tell my story.

I am still here.

My testimony remains.

My faith remains.

And I leave revenge to God.

Praise Jesus.
