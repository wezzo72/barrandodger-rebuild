I am Dr Richard William McLean, known publicly as Barran Dodger. I have assembled the Barran Dodger Archive to preserve and organise a substantial body of material concerning my life, research, professional work, correspondence, dealings with institutions, faith and efforts to pursue accountability and practical security. PART I — MY TESTIMONY 1. I AM THE PERSON WHO LIVED THIS HISTORY My name is Dr Richard William McLean. I am Barran Dodger. I hold a PhD from Victoria University, awarded in 2020. I wrote Recovered, Not Cured, published by Allen & Unwin in 2003, and I have worked as an illustrator, news graphics artist and cartoonist. I have spent years documenting what I believe to be a profound failure of institutional responsibility, protection, accountability and basic human decency. I have approached government departments, oversight bodies, financial institutions, disability services, legal assistance services, hospitals, mental-health organisations, universities, publishers, galleries, journalists, advocacy organisations and elected representatives. I have pursued correspondence, complaints, Freedom of Information requests, administrative reviews, workers’ compensation matters and other pathways in an effort to obtain answers and assistance. I have preserved documents because I refuse to allow my life to be reduced to somebody else’s administrative summary. I am not the person who should be embarrassed by this record. The organisations whose conduct I have documented should be prepared to answer for their own words, decisions and actions. I have experienced prolonged insecurity, financial hardship, isolation, difficulty obtaining practical assistance and the exhausting responsibility of trying to preserve a complicated documentary history while living through its consequences. I have had to become the archivist of my own experience. I have organised correspondence, preserved official records, maintained an index, linked documents and tried to make a complicated history intelligible to other people. I have done this because I believe the record matters. I refuse to let institutional language sanitise what I have lived through. I refuse to allow a referral to masquerade as a resolution, an acknowledgement to masquerade as meaningful assistance, or a list of completed processes to be presented as proof that a person’s circumstances have been adequately addressed. I am done writing my life in a language designed to make institutions comfortable. I am telling my story in my own voice.
